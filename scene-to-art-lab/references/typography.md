# Default Poster Typography

Use this typography system for every generated poster unless the user
explicitly requests another direction or no text.

Treat typography as part of the composition rather than a caption placed
after the image is finished. Keep one recognizable visual system across
a series, while allowing scale, position, angle, overlap and reading
direction to respond to each individual scene.

## Unified visual system

Use one modern neo-grotesk sans-serif family throughout the poster.

- Set the primary title in a bold or semibold condensed uppercase style.
- Use tall, narrow proportions, clean geometric construction, even strokes
  and tight but readable tracking.
- Set supporting copy in the same visual family using regular, medium or
  light weights.
- Keep terminals plain and counters open.
- Maintain a contemporary editorial character.
- Avoid sporty, techno, ornamental, handwritten, vintage-display and
  retro-futurist letterforms unless the user explicitly requests them.
- Use one type family, not a mixture of unrelated display fonts.
- Create hierarchy through scale, weight, spacing and placement rather
  than changing font styles.

The system must feel consistent across a series even when individual
poster arrangements differ.

## Primary title

Derive one scene-specific English title from the visible subject,
atmosphere, motion, place, material or visual proposition.

- Use 1–3 words.
- Prefer approximately 4–16 letters in total when possible.
- Use bold or semibold uppercase condensed lettering.
- Make the title the dominant typographic element.
- Preserve user-supplied wording and capitalization exactly.
- Keep every essential letter readable.
- Do not repeat the title decoratively without a compositional reason.
- Do not invent brands, model names, locations, events, specifications,
  dates, credits or slogans.

Prefer concrete editorial language rather than generic filler such as
`DESIGN POSTER`, `CREATIVE ART` or `FUTURE VISION`.

## Editorial micro-copy

Supporting English text is optional. Use it only when it enriches the
poster and enough type-safe space is available.

The poster may contain up to three supporting text groups:

1. a short observational sentence;
2. a short atmospheric or conceptual sentence;
3. a compact metadata-like fragment.

Recommended limits:

- Use 0–3 supporting groups.
- Keep each sentence approximately 4–18 words.
- Keep the total supporting copy below approximately 45 words.
- Keep metadata fragments to 1–5 words.
- Use short paragraphs rather than one large explanatory block.
- Derive every statement from visible image content or the poster
  proposition.
- Do not invent factual information.
- Omit a text group when the image is already visually dense.

Examples of acceptable functions include:

- describing a visible gesture, object, rhythm or spatial relationship;
- extending the atmosphere of the scene;
- identifying a visual theme through short words such as
  `OBJECT / POSE / DELIGHT`;
- creating editorial rhythm through two or three separated text blocks.

Micro-copy exists to enrich hierarchy, not to explain the whole image.

## Image and title interaction

Allow typography to interact actively with the subject.

The title may:

- sit behind a person, object or architectural edge through controlled
  masking;
- cross in front of a non-critical region;
- align with a dominant perspective line;
- be interrupted by one recognizable silhouette;
- extend beyond an image field into negative space;
- stack vertically;
- follow one controlled diagonal;
- crop slightly at a canvas edge when readability remains intact.

Preserve the title's overall readability. Do not hide several essential
letters or place the entire word behind the subject.

Use overlap to strengthen depth and hierarchy, not as a decorative trick.

## Placement variation

Keep the type system consistent while varying the composition according
to the source image.

Possible arrangements include:

- horizontal title across the upper or lower field;
- vertical title along a strong architectural edge;
- stacked title beside a figure;
- title partially embedded in a large object;
- title following a road, stair, wire, shadow or horizon direction;
- asymmetric title balanced by micro-copy on the opposite side;
- title divided across two connected spatial zones.

Use no more than one dominant title direction per poster.

Supporting paragraphs may occupy different positions, but they should
share consistent alignment, scale and spacing. Keep long supporting
sentences horizontal whenever possible. Use vertical or angled micro-copy
only for very short fragments.

Do not force every poster in a series to use the same placement template.

## Scale and hierarchy

Establish a clear typographic hierarchy:

1. one dominant condensed uppercase title;
2. zero to three much smaller supporting text groups;
3. optional compact metadata-like fragments.

Create decisive scale contrast between the title and micro-copy.

The title should be readable at thumbnail size. Supporting text should
become visible at normal viewing size without competing with the focal
subject.

Avoid several medium-sized text elements with equal visual weight.

## Color

Select typography colors from the current source-derived poster palette.

- Prefer one solid high-contrast title color.
- Use one quieter supporting-copy color.
- Permit restrained contrast changes when type crosses different fields.
- Do not carry a title color from a previous poster by habit.
- Avoid outlines, bevels, glows, gradients, chrome, extrusion and heavy
  drop shadows.
- Use registration shifts or ink irregularity only when they belong to
  the selected physical medium.

## Type-safe space

Reserve intentional space for typography during composition planning.

- Treat the type zone as active negative space.
- Give the title enough breathing room.
- Place supporting text where texture and contrast allow legibility.
- Do not squeeze text into leftover corners.
- Do not place long paragraphs over faces, hands or defining product
  details.
- Avoid boxes, banners, capsules and arbitrary text panels unless a
  source-derived graphic structure clearly requires one.

## Text reliability

State every permitted word exactly in the image-generation prompt.

For each text group:

- provide the exact wording;
- specify capitalization;
- specify its hierarchy and approximate position;
- forbid all other lettering;
- prohibit random serial numbers, fake labels, invented credits and
  corrupted filler text.

When exact text is mission-critical and the image model cannot reproduce
it reliably, generate a clean type-safe zone and recommend composing the
final lettering separately.

Prefer fewer accurate words over dense corrupted typography.

## Prompt vocabulary

Describe the title treatment as:

`bold condensed uppercase neo-grotesk sans serif, tall narrow proportions,
clean even strokes, tight readable tracking, editorial scale contrast,
integrated with the subject through controlled masking or overlap,
no text box, no outline, no shadow, no decorative effects`

Describe supporting copy as:

`small clean neo-grotesk editorial micro-copy from the same typography
family, regular or light weight, consistent alignment and spacing,
arranged as restrained short paragraphs or metadata-like fragments`

## Acceptance checks

Before accepting the result, confirm:

- the title is correctly spelled and readable;
- the title relates specifically to the current image;
- the title uses the locked condensed uppercase visual form;
- supporting copy uses the same typography family;
- no more than three supporting text groups appear;
- micro-copy enriches the layout without overpowering the image;
- image and title overlap has a clear compositional purpose;
- the type direction responds to source geometry;
- the series remains typographically consistent without repeating one
  fixed layout;
- no invented brand, date, credit, logo or factual claim appears;
- no corrupted or additional lettering appears;
- sufficient breathing room remains around the typography.
