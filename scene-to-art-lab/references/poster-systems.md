# Poster System Reference

Choose exactly one system after reading the source image. A system is a spatial strategy, not a decorative template. Adapt it to source geometry and the selected medium.

## Monumental crop

Use one identity anchor at oversized scale, allowing it to cross one or two canvas edges. Place the sharpest detail near a third rather than dead center. Oppose the mass with a quiet type-safe zone. Best for vehicles, products, portraits, and iconic architecture.

Avoid showing the entire subject as a centered catalogue image. Let non-critical contours dissolve before or at the crop.

## Asymmetric field

Compress the subject into roughly one-third to one-half of the canvas and make the remaining ground active through color, wash, paper, shadow, or one source-derived graphic echo. Use unequal visual weights that still balance. Best for poetic watercolor, ink-wash, fashion, travel, and contemplative scenes.

Avoid a subject floating weakly in empty space. Give the field a directional pull back toward the focal anchor.

## Diagonal momentum

Align the subject, road, gaze, light, or brush flow to one decisive diagonal. Counter it with a smaller opposing mass or type-safe zone. Best for vehicles, movement, performance, streets, and expressive painting.

Avoid multiple competing diagonals or random speed lines.

## Fragment and echo

Keep one primary source fragment and repeat, offset, mirror, enlarge, or crop one secondary identity cue. Use no more than three echoes and vary their scale or density. Best for pop screenprint, editorial portraiture, products, and rhythmic architecture.

Avoid generic tiled panels. Every echo must reinforce recognition, motion, time, or emphasis.

## Split tension

Create two unequal interlocking regions: factual subject versus transformed field, opaque versus transparent, dense versus empty, or warm versus cool. Let one contour cross the division to bind them. Best for surrealism, hybrids, conceptual portraits, and product narratives.

Avoid a simple before/after split or two unrelated halves.

## Vertical procession

Build a top-to-bottom sequence using scale change, tonal progression, fading marks, or one repeated source cue. Anchor one stage strongly and let the others act as rhythm. Best for tall architecture, figures, landscapes, editorial storytelling, and relief print.

Avoid equal-sized stacked panels or infographic-like boxes.

## Poster hierarchy contract

For every system:

- make the dominant image readable at thumbnail size;
- use one primary focal anchor and at most two supporting graphic devices;
- establish a clear eye path rather than distributing attention evenly;
- reserve an intentional type-safe zone even when the output contains no words;
- treat negative space as a shaped field with tension, not unused margin;
- derive rules, crops, repeats, color blocks, and textures from source contours, light, perspective, or movement;
- avoid mockup borders, ornamental frames, arbitrary labels, fake logos, and generic geometric decoration.
