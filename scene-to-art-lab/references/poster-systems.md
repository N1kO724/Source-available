# Poster System Reference

Choose one primary poster system after reading the source image.

A poster system is a spatial strategy, not a decorative template.
Adapt it to the source geometry, selected artistic medium, title length,
micro-copy density and intended visual energy.

Do not force every photograph into the same layout. Preserve one unified
typography system across a series while varying scale, crop, direction,
overlap and negative-space structure according to each source.

## System selection

Before choosing a layout, identify:

- the primary identity anchor;
- the dominant direction or perspective;
- the strongest area of negative space;
- the natural position for a title;
- whether the scene benefits from stillness, rhythm, tension or motion;
- whether micro-copy will enrich the image or create unnecessary density.

Choose exactly one primary system below.

A secondary device may be borrowed from another system only when it has
a clear function. Do not average several systems together.

## Monumental crop

Enlarge one identity anchor until it becomes the dominant graphic mass.

Allow the subject to cross one or two canvas edges. Place the most
recognizable detail near a third rather than automatically centering it.

Use the remaining field as active negative space for the title or
micro-copy.

Best for:

- portraits;
- vehicles;
- products;
- sculptural objects;
- iconic architecture.

Typography options:

- place a large title behind the enlarged subject;
- allow one contour to interrupt the title;
- align micro-copy with a strong subject edge;
- let the title extend from the subject into exposed paper.

Avoid:

- showing the entire subject like a catalogue image;
- scaling the subject without changing the original composition;
- hiding too many essential title letters;
- resolving every contour equally.

## Asymmetric field

Compress the focal subject into approximately one-third to one-half of
the canvas and let the remaining ground become an active visual field.

Build balance through unequal visual weight rather than centered symmetry.

Best for:

- poetic portraits;
- watercolor;
- ink-wash;
- fashion;
- travel scenes;
- contemplative landscapes.

Typography options:

- place the title in the larger quiet field;
- use two or three separated micro-copy groups to guide the eye;
- align supporting text with a horizon, shadow or architectural edge;
- use restrained vertical text along one side when the source supports it.

Avoid:

- leaving the subject weakly floating in empty space;
- treating negative space as an unused margin;
- placing all copy in one corner;
- using decorative shapes without source evidence.

## Diagonal momentum

Build the composition around one decisive diagonal derived from the
subject, road, stairs, wires, light, gaze, body gesture or brush flow.

Counter the main diagonal with a smaller opposing mass or type zone.

Best for:

- movement;
- streets;
- escalators;
- vehicles;
- performance;
- expressive painting;
- urban photography.

Typography options:

- rotate the title to follow the dominant diagonal;
- use a horizontal micro-copy block as a counterweight;
- let the title begin outside the image field and enter the subject;
- use one short metadata fragment along the directional line.

Avoid:

- inventing a diagonal unrelated to the photograph;
- using several competing angles;
- rotating long paragraphs;
- adding generic speed lines.

## Fragment and echo

Keep one primary source fragment and repeat, offset, mirror, enlarge or
crop one secondary identity cue.

Use no more than three echoes. Vary their scale, opacity or density.

Best for:

- pop-art screenprint;
- editorial portraits;
- products;
- signage;
- patterned architecture;
- rhythmic urban scenes.

Typography options:

- interleave title letters with repeated fragments;
- repeat one short metadata fragment once when it reinforces rhythm;
- use micro-copy between echoes to create editorial pacing;
- align the title with the repetition axis.

Avoid:

- generic tiled panels;
- repeating the entire source photograph;
- equal-sized echoes;
- decorative repetition without meaning.

Every echo must reinforce recognition, motion, time, rhythm or emphasis.

## Split tension

Create two unequal interlocking regions.

Possible contrasts include:

- factual subject versus transformed field;
- opaque versus transparent;
- dense versus empty;
- warm versus cool;
- photographic detail versus printed abstraction;
- stillness versus movement.

Let at least one subject contour or title element cross the division to
connect the two regions.

Best for:

- editorial surrealism;
- conceptual portraits;
- hybrid media;
- products;
- architecture;
- narrative scenes.

Typography options:

- divide the title across both regions;
- change the title color when it crosses the split;
- place micro-copy on the quieter side;
- use one line or edge to connect text and subject.

Avoid:

- a simple before-and-after comparison;
- two unrelated halves;
- equal 50/50 division;
- boxed panels that resemble an infographic.

## Vertical procession

Build a top-to-bottom sequence through scale change, tonal progression,
fading marks or one repeated source cue.

Anchor one stage strongly and let the others act as rhythm.

Best for:

- tall architecture;
- standing figures;
- stairs;
- towers;
- vertical landscapes;
- editorial storytelling;
- relief print.

Typography options:

- stack the title vertically;
- distribute short micro-copy groups at different heights;
- align the title with the primary vertical structure;
- let image detail decrease as text becomes more prominent.

Avoid:

- equal-sized stacked panels;
- infographic-like boxes;
- several unrelated focal points;
- filling every vertical zone with text.

## Type-image interlock

Use the title as a major compositional shape and weave it through the
subject using controlled foreground and background relationships.

The title may pass behind one subject area and in front of another,
provided that the word remains understandable.

Best for:

- portraits;
- fashion;
- bold objects;
- vehicles;
- architecture;
- pop screenprint;
- strong editorial covers.

Typography options:

- mask one or two letters behind the subject;
- allow the subject to emerge through large counters;
- use the title as a crop boundary;
- let the title connect two otherwise separated image regions;
- place micro-copy in the quieter spaces created by the large letters.

Avoid:

- hiding the full title;
- placing every letter on a different depth plane;
- using overlap without improving hierarchy;
- making the title look like an accidental obstruction.

The title must remain readable at thumbnail size.

## Editorial window

Create one dominant image window inside a larger field of paper, pigment,
ink or restrained graphic material.

The image window does not need a literal border. Its edge may dissolve,
tear, overprint or transition naturally into the surrounding field.

Best for:

- landscapes;
- architecture;
- travel;
- quiet documentary scenes;
- image-led editorial posters;
- compositions that need generous breathing room.

Typography options:

- place a large title partially inside and partially outside the image;
- allow the title to crop or reveal the image through its letterforms;
- place short paragraphs below, beside or across the window edge;
- use a small metadata fragment as a distant counterpoint.

Avoid:

- decorative picture frames;
- uniform white borders;
- placing an unchanged photograph inside a rectangle;
- treating the surrounding field as empty padding.

## Title direction

Choose title direction from visible source geometry.

Permitted dominant directions include:

- horizontal;
- vertically stacked;
- rotated 90 degrees;
- one controlled diagonal;
- split across two connected lines;
- partially cropped at a canvas edge;
- aligned to perspective, horizon, stairs, wires, shadow or body gesture.

Use only one dominant title direction per poster.

Use no more than two reading directions across the entire composition.
Supporting paragraphs should normally remain horizontal. Reserve vertical
or angled placement for titles and very short fragments.

Do not rotate text merely to make the poster look experimental.

## Micro-copy placement

Use zero to three supporting text groups.

Possible placements include:

- opposite the focal anchor;
- along a quiet architectural edge;
- inside an area of exposed substrate;
- between repeated image fragments;
- beneath a title baseline;
- at the boundary between image and negative space;
- as a small counterweight near a distant corner.

Keep supporting text subordinate to both the title and focal image.

Maintain consistent size, line spacing and alignment across the poster.
Do not scatter unrelated text across the canvas.

## Series consistency

For a unified poster series, lock:

- the typography family;
- title weight and condensed proportions;
- micro-copy scale range;
- overall material character;
- print or paper behavior;
- hierarchy between title, image and supporting text;
- spelling and text-quality standards.

Allow these elements to vary:

- layout system;
- title position;
- title angle;
- crop and scale;
- image-title overlap;
- micro-copy placement;
- source-derived palette;
- negative-space distribution.

Consistency should come from the visual system, not from repeating one
template.

## Poster hierarchy contract

For every system:

- make the dominant image readable at thumbnail size;
- use one primary focal anchor;
- use one primary layout system;
- use at most two supporting graphic devices;
- reserve an intentional type-safe zone;
- establish a clear eye path;
- create decisive scale contrast;
- treat negative space as a shaped compositional field;
- derive crops, rules, repeats, angles and textures from the source;
- integrate typography with image geometry;
- keep micro-copy subordinate;
- preserve at least one intentional lost edge;
- keep at least one canvas region open or substrate-led.

Avoid:

- mockup borders;
- ornamental frames;
- unchanged centered-photo layouts;
- equal visual weight everywhere;
- arbitrary labels;
- fake logos;
- random serial numbers;
- generic geometric decoration;
- corrupted or additional lettering.

## Acceptance checks

Before accepting the result, confirm:

- one primary poster system clearly organizes the image;
- the system responds to the source rather than imposing a fixed template;
- the focal anchor remains recognizable;
- title direction follows visible source geometry;
- title-image overlap improves depth or hierarchy;
- the title remains readable at thumbnail size;
- supporting copy does not compete with the image;
- no more than three micro-copy groups appear;
- dominant image, title, supporting field and negative space form a clear
  hierarchy;
- at least one area remains open or substrate-led;
- the series feels related without repeating an identical layout;
- no arbitrary frame, fake label, invented logo or unwanted lettering
  appears.
